01 / THE CHALLENGE

A directory can hold the answer.
That doesn't make it easy to find.

FNHA's existing provider information was available through a spreadsheet and a map. Finding support meant sorting through names, interpreting unfamiliar designations, and working out whether a provider was available and appropriate.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

First Nations community members often struggle to find the right mental health provider. Existing provider information is difficult to search, compare, and trust, creating barriers to accessing timely, culturally appropriate care.

OUR DESIGN QUESTION

How might we help FNHA users quickly discover and confidently choose a mental health provider that best matches their needs while reducing cognitive load and making the search experience intuitive and culturally supportive?

04 / TESTING THE DIRECTION

Test the assumptions
before polishing the interface.

We explored two directions: a guided search experience and a redesigned map with a provider list. My contribution included planning user testing. The team compared prototypes and visual directions, looking at how people searched, interpreted filters, and assessed provider information.

Internal test figures reported in the Week 5 and Week 6 presentations. Availability is a survey finding, not a measure of improved access to care.

WHAT WE LEARNED

Filters did the heavy lifting.

40.9% preferred filters, 50% preferred filters with a map, and only 9.1% preferred the map alone. Participants also called out clutter and competing information.

HOW IT SHAPED THE NEXT VERSION

Make the essential signals easier to scan.

We prioritized availability, clearer provider cards, more visible filters, and a calmer mobile hierarchy. The early hybrid direction combined map visibility with a simpler search structure.

05 / THE SCOPE DECISION

A useful idea still has
to be the right thing to build.

After internal testing, we discussed a mobile-first pivot that also considered unreliable connectivity. The proposal explored full internet, low internet, and offline experiences, including saved provider information and a possible SMS fallback.

That exploration changed the conversation, but it did not become the final scope. The client did not need offline mode, so we did not proceed with it. Instead, the team delivered an additional admin website to support provider data management.

EXPLORED · NOT DELIVERED

Offline and SMS support

Considered after internal testing. Offline mode was not pursued following client alignment. SMS and native app distribution remained proposals.

06 / MY MOBILE INTERFACE WORK

room to be obvious.

I designed the mobile interface, focusing on a readable hierarchy and a clear path from searching to reviewing a provider. The team’s testing highlighted tight spacing, small touch targets, confusing filters, and weak feedback around saved providers.

Key Design Decisions

My mobile interface work sat within a shared design process. These decisions connect the team's research and testing with the direction we took.

01 / SEARCH

Give search a clear starting point.

THE SIGNAL

In external testing, people tried broad terms such as “mental health” and “FNHA,” then encountered mismatched or empty results.

THE DECISION

Make the entry point explicitly city-based, with preferences helping people refine the directory.

02 / FILTERS & FIT

Help people narrow choices with confidence.

THE SIGNAL

95.5% of internal respondents valued availability. Later, only 19 of 33 external respondents rated filters intuitive or very intuitive.

THE DECISION

Prioritize availability and clearer preferences. Later documented iterations included simpler filter groups and an age-range slider.

08 / LEARNING AND ITERATING

Something can be helpful
and still be hard to use.

MY TEST-PLANNING CONTRIBUTION

Plan the task, the questions, and what to observe.

I co-planned the external test with Bam. The July 7 plan assigned us responsibility for interview questions, the survey, task cards, prototype and survey checks, and the participant testing flow.

The planned session moved from a welcome and consent to a mobile or desktop task, a questionnaire, and observation notes. The interview prompts explored what information mattered, which filters felt confusing, and what helped people feel confident before contacting a provider.

A SPECIFIC MOBILE DESIGN QUESTION

Would an expandable map clarify the screen—or add another step?

At this prototype stage, mobile used a list with an expandable full-screen map; desktop used a side-by-side map and list. The test plan asked whether people noticed the mobile toggle without prompting, whether the interaction matched their expectations, and whether the extra view helped with efficiency, confidence, or cognitive load.

The external testing round involved 35+ participants, including 3 First Nations users. It exposed a gap between valuing a feature and understanding how to use it, particularly around filters and map-based discovery.

Yet 21 of 33 respondents named filters among the most helpful features. The priority was to improve a valued interaction, rather than remove it.

SEARCH

Broad-looking search created false expectations.

People tried terms such as “mental health” and “FNHA,” then encountered mismatched or empty results. The design direction shifted toward an explicit city search and clearer guidance.

FILTERS

Useful filters still needed predictable logic.

The report identified a specific mismatch: selecting several options in one category behaved like AND, while testers expected OR. Long lists and unfamiliar labels added friction. The report recommended OR within a category; the design research documents simpler groups and an age-range slider among the later improvements.

MAP

Being visible wasn't the same as being useful.

Internal testing had supported map-and-list exploration. The later results report recorded strong average noticeability, but people who found the map still questioned its value compared with routing tools such as Google Maps. The final presentation and design research record its removal from the delivered experience.

GUIDANCE & TRUST

The words around the interface mattered.

Participants valued the tutorial, but needed clearer location labels, more specific availability information, and a less ambiguous way to report incorrect details.

LATER CHANGES DOCUMENTED BY THE TEAM

Turn the feedback into specific interface changes.

  • Make the entry point explicitly city-based and strengthen the landing page hierarchy.
  • Sort providers by availability, and replace age-group checkboxes with an age-range slider.
  • Make referral notices and availability labels clearer on provider cards.
  • Add saved-provider confirmation and a more helpful no-results experience.

These are documented iterations, not evidence of a measured post-redesign uplift.

The report also showed only a modest change in self-reported provider confidence, from 7.64 to 8.06 out of 10 during the task. Completing a search did not fully resolve whether a provider would feel like a good fit before the first contact.

What the results can—and cannot—tell us

This was guided usability testing, with limited representation from the target community. The findings helped identify usability problems; they do not establish clinical outcomes, long-term adoption, or improved access to care.

Research should change the scope.

The value of testing was not just improving a screen. It helped the team reconsider the map, explore connectivity, and align the final work with the client's actual priorities.

Mobile design is an information problem.

Moving a desktop layout onto a phone is only the beginning. The more important work is deciding what deserves attention first and making each next step understandable.

Keep listening beyond the prototype.

With only three First Nations participants in the external round, continued community involvement would be an important next step before making broader claims about cultural appropriateness and impact.
