01 / THE CHALLENGE

Finding a field is only
half the challenge.

For someone new to Metro Vancouver, a casual game can mean searching multiple websites, checking a venue in person, and hoping there are people to play with. Regular athletes face a related problem: schedules and sign-up information are scattered across different venues.

Our team designed GroundLink to bring sports locations, availability information, drop-in games, and player connections into a single mobile concept.

THE DESIGN QUESTION

How might we help people find a suitable place and people to play with, while making the path to joining a game clearer?

02 / DESIGN PROCESS

From a broad idea
to a shared direction.

We moved from an initial health-related idea to recreation and sports after considering the difficulty of researching personal health information within the project. When our team changed from five members to three, I helped maintain alignment through regular check-ins and discussions supported by our instructors.

Discover & define

Review existing ways to find sports grounds; frame the needs through personas and experience maps.

Explore & prototype

Sketch the core tasks, digitise grey-box wireframes, and develop a mid-fidelity prototype. My focus was wireframes, copy, and image selection.

Test & refine

Run three prototype sessions, organise the findings, and refine the interaction flow before high-fidelity delivery.

03 / RESEARCH INSIGHTS

Two needs.
One connected experience.

My early search across app listings and venue websites helped frame the problem: finding a place, understanding whether it is suitable, and organising other players were separate tasks. I distilled the needs and behaviours into two imagined personas, Davis and Samantha.

Davis · Find a game and a community

A newcomer wants casual soccer games and players at a similar skill level. A venue list alone does not answer who he can play with.

Samantha · Plan with confidence

A regular multi-sport player needs clear schedules, sport types, and availability before committing to a venue.

The design implication

Connect location discovery with the next action: checking details, finding a game, or inviting others.

Personas as design tools

Davis and Samantha are imagined personas from the original case study. Their stories and experience maps frame design needs; they are not individual participant records.

04 / STRUCTURING THE EXPERIENCE

Start with the ground.
Connect it to the game.

The primary flow focused on finding fields, checking schedules, booking in advance, and inviting friends. A secondary flow explored discovering players, viewing profiles, and direct messaging.

I contributed grey-box wireframes, copywriting, and image selection to make these tasks tangible before the team refined the interface.

05 / TESTING & ITERATION

A completed task can still
hide a design problem.

Our three mid-fidelity test participants completed the assigned tasks, but their feedback still identified opportunities to improve privacy, workflow clarity, visual style, usability, usefulness, and prototype interactions.

Privacy · Reconsider nearby-player discovery

The iteration plan removed the nearby-users list and shifted toward groups and invitation posts for finding players.

Workflow · Make game management visible

The plan added frames showing how to find a created game, manage participants, invite friends, and set or end its time period.

Consistency · Carry filters across pages

Ground, Game, and Player pages needed consistent tag filtering, including removing each selected tag individually.

What the test established

The original account reports task completion by all three participants. This small prototype test informed iteration; it does not establish improved booking speed, adoption, or live venue availability.

06 / INTERFACE DESIGN

A clearer way to discover
where to play.

The team’s final high-fidelity screens bring ground recommendations, sports categories, and venue details into a consistent mobile layout. My contribution to the shared Figma styles supported this consistency across the prototype.

07 / KEY DESIGN DECISIONS

Make the next action
easier to understand.

These decisions connect the team’s research and testing with my work on content, wireframes, and interface consistency.

01 · Connect discovery with venue details

The personas needed more than a location name. Ground information, sport context, and the next action belong in the same journey.

02 · Treat social discovery as a privacy decision

The testing feedback challenged a nearby-player list. Group and invitation concepts offered another direction for connecting around an activity.

03 · Build a shared UI vocabulary

I helped create effect, colour, and font styles in Figma. A green primary colour, orange highlights, and Noto Sans gave the team a consistent starting point for high-fidelity work.

08 / DELIVERY & SCOPE

A prototype for a more
connected sporting routine.

We delivered a high-fidelity mobile prototype and a project video. The concept connects ground discovery, venue information, game organisation, and finding other players.

Prototype scope

Availability and booking are represented in the design concept. The project account does not document a production service connected to live court schedules or payment systems.

09 / MY CONTRIBUTION & REFLECTION

From individual screens
to a shared design language.

My contribution was to make the team’s direction easier to communicate and build upon: writing the personas, shaping grey-box screens and copy, supporting the Figma style library, and editing the project video.

Use content to clarify the task

Writing personas and interface copy helped me connect a user’s goal with what a screen needs to explain.

Make consistency part of the workflow

Creating reusable Figma styles taught me to support the whole prototype, alongside the individual screens.

Keep collaboration concrete

As the team changed, regular conversations helped us revisit scope and agree on what to build next. For a future iteration, I would prioritise testing the revised privacy and game-management flows.
